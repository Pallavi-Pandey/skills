# 4. Software as a Service (SaaS)

**SaaS** delivers a complete application through a browser, mobile app, or API. Email, customer-relationship management, collaboration suites, and learning-management systems are familiar examples. The provider operates the application; users configure it and consume its functions.

SaaS has the lowest infrastructure burden and is often the fastest route to business value. The trade-off is limited customization and dependence on the vendor's roadmap, availability, data-export options, and security controls.

## Security is configuration plus governance

SaaS security commonly fails through weak identity practices rather than a broken hypervisor: no MFA, excessive administrator roles, public sharing links, or former employees retaining access. Before adoption, evaluate identity integration, audit logs, data location, retention, backups, incident notification, and contract exit terms.

---

## Data portability and continuity

Before placing critical records in SaaS, establish how data can be exported in a usable format and what happens if the service is unavailable. A continuity plan may include offline exports, alternative communication channels, and tested restoration procedures. Provider backups do not automatically mean a customer can recover from accidental deletion.

## Configuration is part of the system

SaaS products are configured systems, not merely websites. Role definitions, sharing policies, retention rules, identity federation, approved integrations, and administrator accounts determine the actual security posture. Test integrations, review privileged roles regularly, and remove access when staff or students leave.

Evaluate availability commitments, support response, data residency, compliance reports, API/export support, and incident-notification processes. The aim is to know which risks the organization accepts and which compensating controls it needs.
